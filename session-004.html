The Big Idea

A Referee, Not a Savior

Almost everything that goes wrong in a political conversation goes wrong because someone has asked politics to do a job it cannot do. A referee keeps the game fair and civil. A referee does not redeem anybody.

When we expect government to save us, every election becomes a last judgment and every opponent becomes an enemy of the good. When we let government be a referee — God's preserving work, keeping society ordered so people can live peacefully — the temperature drops, and an actual conversation becomes possible.

When politics is treated as the thing that saves, it has to expand to cover everything — because salvation covers everything. That is why so many people now feel that every subject, every purchase, and every friendship carries a political charge.

Two-Kingdom Citizens have a way out of that: we already have a Savior, so politics is free to be something smaller and more useful.

It does not mean going quiet, avoiding hard subjects, or pretending you have no convictions.

It does mean refusing to treat a temporal question as a salvation question — so you can discuss it honestly with someone who disagrees, without either of you feeling your soul is on the line.

The goal isn't to win an argument or force policy on others, but to have open, honest dialogue with our friends and neighbors.

The Heart of Session 4

Five Principles for Talking Politics

Two-Kingdom Citizens apply these five principles when the conversation turns to politics or culture. Click any principle to go deeper.

1

Always Differentiate How God Works in the World

Before anything else, know which kingdom you are standing in. God is doing two different things, and confusing them is what makes political conversation explode.

God's Saving Work

The Gospel — Jesus saving people from sin. This is about grace and faith, not government policy.

God's Preserving Work

God using government, family, and society to keep the world ordered and safe. This is where moral law, protecting life, and fairness are discussed.

Ask this instead: not "how do we win?" but "What does God want for our society so that people can live peacefully?"

2

Focus on Values, Not Personalities

Arguments get heated when they attach to a politician or a personality. Shift the ground back to what is actually at stake: values and principles (what you believe is right, and why), policies rather than people (what a law does, not who proposed it), and common ground — the shared moral values, aligned with natural law, that you hold even with people who disagree with you.

3

Be Careful with Language

The way we define terms matters. Whoever controls the language often controls the argument. Be clear about what you mean, avoid loaded terms that trigger immediate conflict, and stick to clear, descriptive language everyone can follow.

4

Defend Specific Liberties

Depoliticizing is not going silent. There are a few non-negotiable issues the church should stand for in the public square — foundational to civil society and human flourishing.

Religious Liberty

The right of everyone to follow their conscience.

Sanctity of Life

Protecting all human life, at any age or ability.

Marriage

Maintaining the traditional definition of family.

Educational Freedom

Families educating their children by their own values.

5

Lead with Kindness

The most important part of these conversations is the relationship you have with the other person. If you care about someone, you will treat them with respect even when you disagree. You don't have to change their mind to have a successful conversation. Share your perspective with genuine concern for their well-being, listen to their side, and remember that our common humanity is more important than political policy.

Apprentice Roundtable

Discussion Questions

Work through these together. Leader notes are hidden by default — let the group wrestle with the question first, then reveal them if it helps.

Question 1 · Balancing Perspectives

The session suggests we should treat politics as a way for government to keep order rather than a way to save the world. How can people focus on shared values instead of arguing over political policies?

Leader Notes

Focus on shared values by identifying moral foundations — often called "natural law" — that are written in nature itself, which you may hold in common even with people who have different religious or philosophical views. By prioritizing these broad moral principles over specific political policies or platforms, you can find more common ground.

Question 2 · Defining Roles

In the Two Kingdoms view, the role of government is to act like a referee rather than a player. Why might it be difficult for people to accept this limited role for government in their daily lives?

Leader Notes

Some people struggle with a limited role for government because they believe the government is the primary entity responsible for granting rights and ensuring quality of life. Additionally, they may believe that humanity has the capacity to solve all problems on its own, and often view traditional structures such as family or the church as obstacles rather than helpful institutions.

Question 3 · Communicating Respectfully

Many political conversations become angry because people lose respect for one another. What are some practical ways to have a respectful conversation about serious moral issues, even when you strongly disagree with the other person?

Leader Notes

To have a respectful conversation, speak the truth with genuine concern and love for the person you are talking to. Focus the discussion on values and moral principles rather than attacking personalities or arguing over political platforms. It is also important to allow for nuance, listen to the other side, and try to understand the reasoning behind why you disagree.
