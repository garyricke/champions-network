The wording fix, as built

This is shipped, not proposed. Every swap below is live in the template and in the rebuilt Boulder pieces as of this morning.

He set three constraints at once, which is why it read like a knot:

  • Undergrads are adults — calling only the congregation members “adults” quietly says they aren’t.
  • “Young adults” against “older adults” is the same mistake with extra words.
  • He wants high schoolers to consider the training, so anything anchored on adult pushes them out of the frame.
The move: stop sorting people by age, sort them by where they come from

The real distinction in that room isn’t old and young. It’s the students who host and the neighbors who come. That is true of everyone present, it says nothing about anyone’s age, and it’s the thing Pastor Jarvis actually wants to communicate — that turning up is a service to the students. The poster already talks this way in its subhead: “the students who host it are asking their neighbors to sit at the table with them.” So this isn’t a new voice, it’s finishing the one the piece already has.

The exact swaps

The word appeared in four phrases across nine places. All of them lived in the template or the chapter file, so fixing them there fixed every chapter after Boulder too.

Poster “Adults from neighboring congregations are what make it hold.” → “Members of neighboring congregations are what make it hold.”
Handouts & landing “Be the adults who show up every time…” → “Be the ones who show up every time, so the students are never the only ones in the room.”
Handouts & landing “bring adults from area churches with you” → “bring members of area congregations with you”
Student handout & social “adults from around Boulder standing beside you” → “neighbors from around Boulder standing beside you”
And one line to bring the high schoolers in

The audience is already stated in the fine print under the QR code. It just needs three words:

Was: “Open to University of Colorado students and to members of neighboring Lutheran congregations.”

Now: “Open to University of Colorado students, high schoolers, and members of neighboring Lutheran congregations.”
On “is congregational member too bulky?”

Yes for a headline, no for the fine print — so use both, in the places each one fits. “Neighbors” where it has to be short and warm; “members of neighboring congregations” where it has to be exact. Nobody has to read the bulky version to understand the invitation, and nobody who needs the precise version is left guessing.

One more, while we’re here

The two handouts were named adult and student internally, which is how the word got into the conversation in the first place. They are now Congregations and Students — in the file names, on the download buttons and in the admin page — because that is where wording leaks back in after it has been fixed on the artwork.

Letting Doctrinal Review approve a page

Step 4 is the one gate nobody on our side controls, and today it stayed that way. Two things came out of the conversation — one settled, one worth chasing.

Settled: send them in batches

Mark’s standing approach is to give Doctrinal Review three or four sessions at a time. If the queue takes as long as it takes, batching stops that from becoming a delay on every single session — it becomes one delay per batch.

Worth chasing: could they review the page itself?

Today the flow is: Gary makes a PDF → Mark emails it → a reviewer downloads it, marks it up, writes an email back. Every one of those steps is friction that has nothing to do with doctrine. A review link instead would mean:

  • Opens on a phone or a computer, nothing to download
  • Approve with a button, or type notes inline against the part in question
  • Possibly record a spoken note, for a reviewer who’d rather talk than type
  • No email to compose, no file to keep track of, no version to reconcile afterwards

It also fixes a quieter problem: with a PDF, approved changes have to be applied twice — once to the document and once to the live page. If the page is what gets reviewed, they can’t drift apart.

Mark will ask, and expects about a week for an answer. He can’t ask the reviewers directly — they’re anonymous by design. His read: the younger ones would welcome it, the older ones are an unknown. Worth framing the ask as fewer steps for them rather than as new technology.

If the answer is no, nothing is lost — the PDF path already works and stays. This only ever removes friction where they’re willing to have it removed.

Pairing an LAA episode with each session

This one is Mark’s idea, not ours — which is why it reads unfamiliar. It’s his own line in Wednesday’s email: “Gary might AI Compute which LAA Podcast might be a complementary resource and suggested extra experience per the session.”
What LAA is

Liberty Action Alert — the LCRL podcast Dr. Seltz hosts. We already built a page around one series of it: four episodes from August 2023 in which Dr. Seltz interviews Mark about the Champions Network. They’re on the site now with players, transcripts and pull-quotes.

  • Part 1 — What Is the Champions Network? (Aug 9, 2023)
  • Part 2 — Why Now? The Case for Organizing (Aug 16)
  • Part 3 — Standing in the Gap (Aug 23)
  • Part 4 — How to Join & What to Expect (Aug 30)
What Mark is proposing

That every Academy session carries an optional “go deeper” listen alongside its Mom & Pop Paper reading. The session teaches the topic; the episode lets a student hear Dr. Seltz and Mark talking through the same ground in conversation, which lands differently from a lecture. Since the transcripts already exist, matching an episode to a session topic is quick and cheap — that’s the “AI compute” part, and it’s the easy bit.

The catch worth raising
We only have four episodes, and all four are about the Network itself. They pair naturally with Session 001 — the introduction — and with very little else. There’s nothing here to match against a session on, say, parental rights or two-kingdom citizenship in the schools.

LAA is an ongoing LCRL program, so there is almost certainly a far bigger back catalogue that simply hasn’t been shared with us. The question for Mark: how many LAA episodes exist, and where do they live? With a real archive this step is genuinely valuable and costs almost nothing per session. With four episodes it runs dry immediately, and we should either drop it from the workflow or reduce it to a one-off pointer on the introductory session.
